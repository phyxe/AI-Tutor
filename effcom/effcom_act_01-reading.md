# Effective Communication review: reading and worked examples

This guide reviews the concepts from the start of Term 2 through the second recovery set. Read it with `effcom_act_01-source-packet.md`, which contains the complete original classroom passages A, B, and C. Those passages are instructional proposals, not official policy or measured research findings. This guide's cases and numbers are also fictional. They do not describe your class.

The worked examples teach a method. They are not your answers to the activity. Your tutor will give a different case or ask you to apply the method to the assigned passage. It may explain the concept and correct a mistaken principle, but it must not supply the target-task answer, calculate the target results for you, or write your synthesis/reflection. Short initial answers are welcome; develop the reason when needed.

## R1. Messages fit a situation

Academic communication builds or examines understanding. Technical communication helps an audience use specialized information or carry out a process. They can overlap. Classify by purpose and features rather than appearance or difficult vocabulary.

Ask: Who is the audience? What should they understand or do? What context affects them? Who/what is the source? Which mode carries meaning? Audience and purpose shape content, organization, tone, detail, and mode. A source identifies origin; a mode identifies form. Multimodal communication combines forms such as words, images, sound, or data displays.

Worked example M1: A workshop card says, “Before connecting the projector, switch off the power and check the cable.” Its immediate purpose is technical: help workshop users act safely in a sequence. The short commands fit action under time constraints. This does not establish that any student actually performed the steps.

## R2. Understand before responding

Use `orient → prepare → capture → check → respond → reflect`. Orient to source and task; prepare relevant prior knowledge/questions; capture the main point and essential supports; check notes against the source; respond with a supported explanation; reflect on the strategy and next adjustment.

A topic names the subject. A main point states what the source says about it. Supporting details explain or substantiate the point. An inference goes beyond explicit wording but needs a reasonable evidence link. Evaluation judges using a criterion. Personal response is not a substitute for source meaning.

Use Point–Evidence–Connection: make the point, identify relevant source evidence, explain the connection. Preserve qualifications such as may, some, under these conditions, or proposed.

Worked example M2: “A dated equipment list helps a club notice changes, but members must still report missing items.” The topic is equipment records. The point is that a dated list supports checking but does not replace reports. “The list prevents every loss” exceeds the source.

## R3. Data and vocabulary require checking

Read the title, labels, units, time period, scale, and denominator before comparing. An observation describes displayed data. Interpretation explains what a pattern might mean within limits. A causal claim needs more than a difference or association.

Worked example M3 — simulated completion data: Group A completed 12 of 20 assigned checks; Group B completed 15 of 20. Rates are 60% and 75%. The difference is 15 percentage points, not automatically a 15% relative increase. Different completion counts do not prove a method caused improvement. Check equal denominators before comparing counts.

For unfamiliar terms, identify the term and sentence context, propose a meaning, use word parts if helpful, verify against a supplied definition or appropriate reference, then reread. A plausible guess is not verified meaning.

Glossary: denominator = the total/reference quantity used in a fraction or rate; completion rate = completed eligible items divided by the specified total; qualification = wording restricting a claim; fidelity = preserving a source's meaning and limits; cohesion = visible language links between ideas; synthesis = an explanation organized around relationships among sources.

## R4. Procedures include monitoring

A process describes how something happens; a procedure directs action toward an outcome. A usable procedure includes prerequisites/materials, ordered actions, conditions or decision points, checks, and completion evidence. Monitoring means comparing progress with the criterion and adjusting rather than merely following a list.

Worked example M4: Before uploading a report, open the required file, check that its title and date match the assignment, upload it, then verify that the confirmation identifies the correct file. If the wrong filename appears, correct the selection before claiming submission. A progress bar alone does not prove the correct report was submitted. A useful clarification names the missing decision: “Which date should appear on the final report?”

## R5. Discussion develops ideas

Accountable discussion respects people, uses evidence, and explains reasoning. Recitation supplies expected answers; cumulative talk agrees without examining; disputational talk opposes without productive reasons; exploratory talk clarifies, connects, tests, and develops claims.

Restate an idea fairly before challenging it. Questions can clarify meaning, request evidence, probe reasoning, test a limit, connect ideas, or examine consequences. Useful feedback identifies the criterion, the current issue, and a next step. Accept, adapt, or reject a suggestion with a reason tied to evidence or purpose. Collaboration coordinates work but does not establish each person's understanding automatically.

Worked example M5: “Your recommendation names the action. Explain which source detail supports it, so readers can assess your reason.” This targets the work and gives a next move. “You are bad at explaining” attacks the person without a repair. A chat practice response is not evidence that you participated in an observed group discussion.

## R6. Transform a source faithfully

Notes retain useful ideas with source identity. An outline shows hierarchy. A synopsis gives a compact overview. A summary condenses main meaning and essential limits. A paraphrase restates a selected idea using independently constructed wording/structure while keeping meaning. Changing a few words while copying sentence structure is patchwriting. Both paraphrases and summaries require acknowledgment; citation does not make copying acceptable. A direct quotation preserves exact words, quotation marks, and a real location where available.

Worked example M6: Original fictional sentence: “The storage checklist reminds staff to inspect labels, but it cannot confirm the condition of unopened containers.” Faithful condensation: “A checklist supports label checks without establishing the condition inside sealed containers.” It retains the boundary. “Checklists guarantee safe storage” distorts the source. Use the activity's source packet for your own work, not this model sentence.

## R7. Synthesis connects sources

Synthesis answers a guiding question by organizing sources around agreement, difference, qualification, condition, or complementary information. Separate summaries alone are not a synthesis. Use a relationship matrix: source claim → relationship → bounded implication for the question.

Worked example M7 — two fictional teaching claims: one recommends large labels so users can locate supplies; another warns that labels must be updated when contents change. Together they support visible, maintained labels, not a claim that large labels alone prevent mistakes. The connecting inference belongs to the writer and must not be presented as an experimentally measured result.

For packet A/B/C, identify the actual relationship yourself. Do not treat a recommendation as measured proof of effectiveness or assume any passage describes your school.

## R8. Acknowledgment links claims to sources

Cite borrowed ideas as well as quotations. A narrative citation makes the author/body part of the sentence; a parenthetical citation puts author/body and date in parentheses. Every cited work must have a matching accurate reference, and listed works must actually be used. A reference identifies the work; it does not prove its claims are correct.

The supplied packet provides its actual instructional source records and sample in-text forms. Its shared registry uses 2026a for A, 2026b for C, and 2026c for B, based on alphabetical title order. Passage letters A/B/C are not year suffixes. For this review use all three packet records and preserve that supplied registry consistently. Do not invent authors, journals, DOI links, page numbers, or publication findings. Direct quotation locations must be real, such as the identified source and paragraph.

## R9. Write and revise for meaning

Choose audience, purpose, and a controlling idea before drafting. Organize by relationships rather than one unrelated source per paragraph. A paragraph needs a focused point, relevant evidence, and an explanation of the connection. Cohesion makes links clear through reference words, repeated key terms, and accurate transitions. A transition cannot repair a false relationship.

Revise meaning, evidence boundaries, relevance, and organization before editing punctuation. Claims should match evidence strength: a passage can recommend an approach without proving universal effectiveness. Keep your initial attempt and explain the revision decision.

Worked example M9: “The sign is useful. However, it helps users locate the room.” If the second statement supports rather than contrasts the first, “however” misrepresents the relationship. Repair the logic/link; do not add formal vocabulary just to sound academic.

## R10. Reflect using evidence

Identify what you can now do, point to an actual answer or correction, and name a specific next step. “I learned everything” does not show strategy or progress. A supported revision made after help is valuable evidence, but it is different from an independently correct first attempt. Preparation and written review do not certify an actual live performance.

## Student task bank: separate from worked examples

These fictional tasks are the assigned review stimuli, not real policies or research. The activity file defines evidence criteria; it does not require copying this whole guide into a submission.

T1 — communication situation: A library orientation card for first-time visitors says, “Use the shelf map to locate your section. Ask the desk volunteer if the label differs from the map.” Explain its purpose, audience/context, source/mode distinction, and one wording choice.

T2 — comprehension: “A reading log can help a learner locate a confusing passage. The log records questions, but questions still need checking against the text.” State the main point, one support, and why “keeping a log guarantees comprehension” is unsupported. Use Point–Evidence–Connection in your explanation.

T3 — simulated data: In one practice session, Group X completed 18 of 30 checks; Group Y completed 12 of 15. Explain the rates and a fair comparison, distinguish percent from percentage points, and state why the display cannot prove the cause. Explain denominator using this case and the glossary, then identify the clue/check used.

T4 — procedure: “Select a file, upload it, and close the page.” Repair the procedure by adding a relevant prerequisite, verification and conditional action. Explain what evidence would establish completion, and ask one precise clarification if a needed assignment detail is unknown. No upload or unsafe physical task is required.

T5 — discussion: In response to “Optional links can supplement a short printed packet,” a peer says, “So you want all internet resources banned.” Fairly restate the actual claim; ask one productive question and give respectful corrective feedback with a reason. Explain how you would accept, adapt, or reject the peer's interpretation. This is written rehearsal, not observed group participation.

T6 — condensation: Read Source C in the companion packet. Write your own concise summary or paraphrase preserving its main idea and qualification, with acknowledgment. Explain how you checked fidelity and how summary differs from paraphrase. Do not condense a worked-example sentence.

T7 — synthesis planning: Read Sources A and B and revisit C. For the question “What makes a required learning packet useful when access conditions vary?”, identify a relationship among the sources, the evidence for it, and one limit. State a student-developed controlling idea; do not request a ready-made synthesis from the tutor.

T8 — citation: Use the supplied source records to write one accurate narrative citation and one parenthetical citation for different packet sources. List all three references consistently with the packet registry. Explain which source supports one of your claims and why a citation alone cannot establish local conditions. Do not invent publication details.

T9 — short source-based explanation: Write a brief connected response to T7's question for a Grade 11 classmate, using the three passages accurately with acknowledgment. No fixed paragraph/word quota. Explain your organization and one source limit. Revisit and revise one real issue identified in your attempt; if it is already sound, explain the specific checks supporting retention rather than inventing a flaw. Retain the original attempt.

T10 — review reflection: Identify one demonstrated strength with an actual answer/correction, one area needing further practice, and the strategy you will use next. Refer to evidence from this review, not unobserved class performance.
