# Unit 2 Reference Chapter: Chapter 1—Title, Background, Questions, Scope, and Statement of the Problem

## Chapter Overview

Unit 1 helped you move from a broad issue to a feasible quantitative research topic. Unit 2 turns that topic into the first formal foundation of your research paper. You will write and align five closely connected parts: the research title, background of the study, research questions, scope and delimitation, and statement of the problem.

These parts should not be written as separate pieces that happen to appear in the same chapter. They must describe the same study. The title gives readers a compact view of the study. The background explains the issue, evidence, and reason for conducting it. The research questions state exactly what the study will answer. The scope and delimitation establish its boundaries. The statement of the problem brings the main problem and specific questions together.

The central alignment chain for this unit is:

`problem -> purpose -> variables -> population and setting -> research questions -> scope -> title`

You may move back and forth while drafting. A question may reveal that a title is too broad. A scope decision may show that a variable cannot be measured. A background source may help you state the problem more accurately. Revision is therefore part of research thinking, not simply correction of grammar.

This chapter uses Creswell's educational research and research design books as foundational references for research problems, purpose, questions, variables, and quantitative alignment. It extends that foundation with Booth and colleagues' discussion of meaningful research problems, Saunders and colleagues' guidance on research ideas and questions, APA quantitative reporting standards, peer-reviewed writing guidance, university writing-center resources, and official Philippine evidence from DepEd, PSA, TESDA, and DOST–FNRI. The Philippine reports provide examples of how public evidence can help frame a local question. They do not prove that a national pattern exists in a particular school.

## Key Concepts

| Concept | Plain Meaning |
|---|---|
| Research problem | A condition, uncertainty, gap, or practical issue that calls for systematic investigation |
| Research title | A concise and accurate name that identifies the central content of the study |
| Background of the study | The section that explains the context, specific problem, relevant evidence, unresolved local question, and rationale for the study |
| Research gap | What relevant knowledge or evidence is still missing, uncertain, incomplete, or unavailable for the study's specific context |
| Rationale | The reason the proposed study deserves to be conducted |
| Purpose | What the study intends to describe, compare, relate, or test |
| Statement of the problem | The organized presentation of the main research problem and the specific questions the study will answer |
| Main problem | The overall question or problem that unifies the study |
| Specific research question | A focused, answerable question tied to data that the study will collect and analyze |
| Scope | What the study deliberately covers |
| Delimitation | A boundary deliberately chosen by the researchers to keep the study focused and feasible |
| Limitation | A constraint or weakness that cannot be fully controlled and may affect the interpretation of findings |
| Locale | The place or institutional setting where the study is conducted |
| Population | The larger group to which the study is directed |
| Respondents or participants | The people who actually provide data |
| Alignment | The condition in which all major parts describe the same study and support one another |

## Detailed Discussion

### 0. Unit 2 Is About Alignment

A research paper can contain grammatically correct sentences and still be technically weak. This happens when its parts do not match.

Consider this example:

**Title:** *Social Media Use and Academic Performance Among Grade 12 Learners*
**Research question:** What social media platform do learners prefer?
**Scope:** The study covers Grade 11 HUMSS learners.
**Data source:** A questionnaire about time management.

Each part sounds research-related, but the study is not aligned. The title promises two variables—social media use and academic performance—and a Grade 12 population. The question asks only about preferred platforms. The scope changes the population to Grade 11 HUMSS. The instrument measures time management, which is not clearly one of the title variables.

An aligned version might look like this:

**Title:** *Daily Social Media Use and Self-Reported Study Time Among Grade 12 Learners*
**Main question:** What is the relationship between daily social media use and self-reported study time among Grade 12 learners in the selected school?
**Scope:** The study covers selected Grade 12 learners enrolled during the stated semester and measures daily social media use and self-reported study time through an approved questionnaire.

The second version is not automatically a perfect study. It still needs a suitable sampling plan, instrument, and analysis. However, its early parts describe the same variables and population.

Creswell and Creswell emphasize consistency among the research problem, purpose, questions or hypotheses, and methods. APA quantitative reporting standards also treat the problem, questions, and design as connected reporting elements (Appelbaum et al., 2018). For Grade 12 researchers, the practical rule is straightforward:

> Every important word in the title should have a clear place in the questions and scope. Every specific question should lead to data that the planned study can ethically obtain and analyze.

### 1. From a Topic to a Research Problem

A topic is an area of interest. A research problem explains what is uncertain, incomplete, difficult, inconsistent, or worth investigating within that area.

| Topic | Possible Research Problem |
|---|---|
| Internet access | Learners use different devices and connection arrangements, but the school lacks local evidence about the access conditions used for online tasks |
| Work immersion | Learners complete work immersion, but their perceived readiness for intended career pathways may vary by specialization |
| Reading | Learners use different research-reference materials, but the relationship between their use of those materials and performance on a defined PR2 comprehension task is unclear |
| Physical activity | Learners report different activity patterns, but the relationship between activity frequency and a non-clinical school-life measure is unknown in the selected group |

Booth et al. (2024) explain that productive research moves from a topic to a question and then to the significance of answering it. A beginning researcher should be able to complete this sentence honestly:

`I am studying __________ because I want to find out __________ so that __________ can better understand __________.`

Example:

`We are studying the internet-access arrangements of Grade 12 learners because we want to find out which arrangements they use for online learning tasks so that the school can better understand the access conditions of the selected learners.`

The last part is the “so what.” It does not need to promise a major policy change. A Grade 12 study may be useful because it provides a clear local description, tests whether two variables are associated, compares defined groups, or supplies baseline information for a teacher or school decision.

#### 1.1 A research problem is not merely a complaint

Weak statement:

`Many students are lazy and do not submit their tasks.`

Problems with this statement include:

- “lazy” is a judgment rather than a measured variable;
- “many” is unsupported;
- it assumes a cause before evidence is collected;
- it does not define which students or tasks are involved.

Safer problem framing:

`The selected Grade 12 sections use online learning tasks, but the frequency of on-time submission and the access conditions learners report have not yet been described for the planned study period.`

The safer version identifies what is not yet known without blaming learners or claiming a cause.

#### 1.2 A research gap is not always “no study exists”

Students often write, “There are no studies about this topic.” That is a risky claim. A limited search cannot prove that no study exists anywhere.

A research gap may instead mean:

- previous studies examined a different population;
- available evidence is national or regional, but the local school situation is not described;
- earlier evidence measured a related but different variable;
- findings from earlier studies are inconsistent;
- a current condition has changed since the earlier data were collected;
- the school needs a practical baseline for a specific decision;
- the relationship between two locally relevant variables remains uncertain.

Safer gap language includes:

- “Local evidence for the selected school remains limited.”
- “The national report does not describe the conditions of learners in this school.”
- “Previous studies have examined internet access, but the planned study focuses specifically on connection stability during online learning tasks.”
- “Available records describe submission rates but do not explain how these rates vary with the access variable examined in this study.”

### 2. The Research Title

The research title is the compact label of the study. It helps readers predict the study's main variables or phenomenon, population, and sometimes its setting or design.

A good quantitative title is:

- accurate;
- specific enough to identify the study;
- concise without hiding essential information;
- aligned with the research questions;
- free from unsupported causal claims;
- written in clear academic language; and
- feasible in relation to the actual population and data source.

Bowman and Kinnan (2018) emphasize clarity, meaningful keywords, correct grammar, and avoidance of unnecessary jargon and abbreviations. Their guidance comes from scientific publishing, so suggested word counts should not become rigid school rules. A slightly longer accurate title is better than a short title that misrepresents the study.

#### 2.1 What a title may contain

Depending on the study, a title may identify:

- the main variable or variables;
- the relationship or comparison being studied;
- the target population;
- the locale, when it helps define the study;
- the timeframe, when it is essential; and
- the design, when the design adds useful clarity.

Example:

`Internet-Connection Stability and Reported On-Time Submission of Online Learning Tasks Among Grade 12 Learners`

This title identifies:

- variable 1: internet-connection stability;
- variable 2: reported on-time submission;
- task context: online learning tasks; and
- population: Grade 12 learners.

The title does not claim that internet stability causes submission behavior.

#### 2.2 Flexible title patterns

Title patterns can help you draft, but they are not formulas that guarantee quality.

**Descriptive study**

`[Variable or condition] among [population] in [setting, if needed]`

Example:

`Perceived Career Readiness Among Grade 12 TVL Learners After Work Immersion`

**Comparative study**

`[Outcome variable] among [population] by [grouping variable]`

Example:

`Perceived Career Readiness Among Grade 12 TVL Learners by Specialization`

**Relationship study**

`[Variable 1] and [Variable 2] among [population]`

Example:

`Use of the Unit 4 PR2 Reference Chapter and Comprehension-Task Performance Among Grade 12 Learners`

**Intervention or experimental study**

`Effect of [intervention] on [outcome] among [population]`

Use this pattern only when the study actually introduces an intervention and uses a design capable of examining its effect. A one-time survey should not use “effect,” “impact,” “influence,” or “causes” simply because two variables are included.

#### 2.3 Title words that require caution

| Risky Word | Why It Can Be Risky | Safer Choice When Appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Implies an intervention or causal conclusion | Relationship, association, difference |
| Impact | Often implies a broad or causal consequence | Reported outcome, perceived contribution, association |
| Influence | May suggest causation | Relationship or association |
| Factors affecting | Claims several variables cause an outcome | Variables associated with; selected correlates of |
| Effectiveness | Requires a defined intervention, comparison, and criterion | Perceived usefulness, satisfaction, performance after—but only if these match the data |
| Problems encountered | Can become vague and overly broad | Frequency or severity of specifically defined challenges |

#### 2.4 Common title problems

**Too broad:**
`Social Media and Students`

The variables, purpose, population, and setting are unclear.

**Too crowded:**
`The Effects of Social Media, Internet, Mobile Games, Family Income, Motivation, Sleep, Food, Transportation, and Teacher Behavior on All Academic Outcomes of Senior High School Students`

This title promises more variables and evidence than a Grade 12 project can realistically manage.

**Unsupported causal claim:**
`The Effect of Social Media on the Grades of Students`

If the study is only a survey, it cannot isolate social media as the cause of grades.

**Unclear population:**
`Study Habits and Quiz Performance`

The title needs the group being studied and possibly the relevant subject or context.

**Unnecessary opening words:**
`A Study of...`, `An Investigation of...`, or `An Assessment of...`

These are not always wrong, but they often add length without adding content.

**Unexplained abbreviation:**
`ICT and OTS Among G12 SHS Learners`

Readers should not have to guess what central title terms mean.

#### 2.5 The title alignment test

Before locking the title, ask:

1. What is the study trying to describe, compare, or relate?
2. Are the central variables or phenomenon visible?
3. Is the population accurately named?
4. Is the setting needed to prevent misunderstanding?
5. Does the title use causal words that the design cannot support?
6. Can the planned instrument actually measure what the title promises?
7. Do the specific research questions use the same central ideas?
8. Is the title narrow enough to complete within the available time?

### 3. The Background of the Study

The background explains why the study exists. It guides readers from a broader context to the specific local question that the research will answer.

The background is not merely a long introduction to the topic. It should establish a logical case:

`context -> specific issue -> relevant evidence -> unresolved question or gap -> rationale -> purpose`

This pattern is consistent with research-introduction guidance that moves from establishing a field or situation, to identifying a problem or gap, to showing how the present study addresses that need. The George Mason University Writing Center describes a similar movement in its guidance for scientific introductions. Booth et al. (2024) likewise connect a research question to the reason answering it matters.

#### 3.1 Context

Context helps readers understand the larger situation. It may introduce:

- a relevant school practice;
- an educational, social, technological, business, health, or technical issue;
- an official policy or program;
- a current pattern shown by credible data; or
- the general importance of the variable being studied.

Context should be relevant. A background about internet access does not need a full history of the internet. A study about work-immersion readiness does not need a long history of vocational education in the Philippines unless that history directly explains the local problem.

#### 3.2 Specific issue

After introducing the context, narrow the discussion to the condition that the study will examine.

Example movement:

- Broad context: learners increasingly use digital tools and online resources.
- Specific issue: access conditions may differ by device, connection type, and location.
- Local uncertainty: the selected school does not yet have evidence about the arrangements Grade 12 learners use for the online tasks covered by the study.

#### 3.3 Relevant evidence

Evidence makes the background traceable. It may come from:

- official statistics;
- peer-reviewed studies;
- credible institutional reports;
- authorized school records;
- policies or program documents; or
- a preliminary needs assessment conducted ethically and reported accurately.

For example, the PSA's 2024 NICTHS provides national and regional information about household internet access, device use, connection types, time online, activities, and barriers. This evidence can establish that digital access is a measurable public issue. It cannot establish the access rate of one particular school.

The correct reasoning is:

`The national survey shows that access conditions vary and can be measured. The national survey does not tell us the exact condition of the selected school. Therefore, a focused local question may be justified.`

The incorrect reasoning is:

`The national survey reports an access problem. Therefore, the same percentage and problem exist among our respondents.`

#### 3.4 Gap or unresolved local question

The gap tells readers what is still not known for the intended study.

A strong local gap is specific:

`Although the NICTHS describes internet access nationally and by region, it does not identify the devices and connection arrangements used by Grade 12 learners in the selected school when completing the online learning tasks included in this study.`

A weak gap is exaggerated:

`No one has ever studied internet access among students.`

#### 3.5 Rationale

The rationale explains why answering the local question may be useful. It should match what the study can realistically contribute.

Reasonable rationale:

`A local description may help the teacher understand which access arrangements should be considered when assigning the selected online tasks.`

Overstated rationale:

`This study will solve the digital divide in the Philippines.`

A small study cannot make that promise.

#### 3.6 Purpose

The end of the background should lead clearly to what the study intends to do.

Examples:

- “This study therefore aims to describe the internet-access arrangements used by selected Grade 12 learners for online learning tasks.”
- “This study aims to examine the relationship between reported connection stability and reported on-time submission of the defined online learning tasks.”
- “This study aims to compare perceived career-readiness scores across the selected TVL specializations.”

The purpose should use a verb that matches the design: describe, compare, examine a relationship, or test a defined intervention.

### 4. Using Philippine Official Evidence Correctly

Official Philippine reports are valuable because they provide real definitions, measurements, and public conditions. They can help students avoid invented claims and empty opening sentences.

However, official evidence must be used with care.

#### 4.1 Digital access: PSA NICTHS

The 2024 NICTHS reports that 48.8% of Philippine households had internet access at home and that 67.3% of individuals aged 10 years and over had used the internet in 2024 (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025b). The survey also reports connection types, devices, locations of access, frequency, time spent online, activities, and barriers.

These figures may support a background about digital access. They do not prove that 48.8% of households represented in a school have home internet. A school-based study should collect its own appropriate local data.

#### 4.2 Literacy and comprehension: PSA FLEMMS

The 2024 FLEMMS distinguishes basic literacy from functional literacy. Its operational definition of functional literacy includes reading, writing, computing, comprehending, integrating information, and making inferences. Final national results reported a 70.8% functional literacy rate among individuals aged 10 to 64 under the survey's methodology (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025a).

This evidence can introduce a discussion about comprehension and literacy. A teacher-made quiz or student questionnaire cannot classify respondents using the official FLEMMS measure. A Grade 12 study should name the exact classroom measure it uses, such as “performance on a teacher-approved PR2 comprehension task.”

#### 4.3 TVL and employment: TESDA SETG

TESDA's 2024 Study on the Employment of TVET Graduates reports employment-related outcomes, job-search experiences, and skills utilization among the graduates covered by the survey (Technical Education and Skills Development Authority, 2025).

This may help frame a TVL or ABM background about career preparation. However, current SHS TVL learners are not automatically equivalent to TESDA graduates. A school study may examine perceived career readiness or work-immersion skill confidence, but it should not treat those perceptions as actual employment or occupational competence.

#### 4.4 Physical activity and health: DOST–FNRI NNS

The 2023 National Nutrition Survey includes nutrition, food consumption, physical activity, behavioral risk factors, and adolescent and adult mental-health indicators (Department of Science and Technology–Food and Nutrition Research Institute, 2023).

These domains may inspire safe, non-invasive student studies. They do not authorize Grade 12 researchers to diagnose health conditions or ask sensitive questions without safeguards. Studies involving mental health, substance use, disordered eating, body weight, or other sensitive personal information require careful ethical review and may be unsuitable for a short school project.

### 5. Writing the Background as a Coherent Argument

A background should read as a connected explanation, not as a collection of copied facts.

#### 5.1 A paragraph-level planning model

A practical background may use the following movement. The number of paragraphs can vary.

**Paragraph or section 1: Establish the context**
Explain the relevant issue and introduce one or two strong sources.

**Paragraph or section 2: Narrow to the specific problem**
Identify the variable, behavior, outcome, group, or local condition that needs attention.

**Paragraph or section 3: Show what available evidence does and does not tell us**
Summarize relevant studies or reports and identify the unresolved local question.

**Paragraph or section 4: State the rationale and purpose**
Explain the reasonable contribution of the study and lead into its purpose and questions.

This is a flexible logic, not a compulsory four-paragraph formula.

#### 5.2 Model background excerpt

The following excerpt demonstrates the reasoning. It is intentionally short and would need additional literature in a full manuscript.

> Internet access is relevant to learning tasks that require students to receive instructions, locate resources, communicate, or submit work online. The 2024 National Information and Communications Technology Household Survey reported national and regional differences in home internet access, connection types, devices, and barriers to use (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025b). These findings show that digital access is not one simple condition; learners may connect through different devices and arrangements.
>
> National data, however, do not describe the exact access conditions of Grade 12 learners in the selected school. For the online tasks covered by the planned study, local information about connection stability and reported on-time submission is not yet available. Without this information, assumptions about learners' access may be inaccurate.
>
> This study therefore aims to examine the relationship between reported internet-connection stability and reported on-time submission of the defined online learning tasks among selected Grade 12 learners. The findings may provide a limited local description that can inform how the participating teacher plans those tasks. The study will not attempt to prove that internet access causes academic performance.

Notice what the model does:

- cites an official source;
- explains the source instead of merely inserting a statistic;
- distinguishes national evidence from local evidence;
- identifies a specific gap;
- gives a realistic rationale;
- states the purpose; and
- limits the claim.

#### 5.3 Background writing problems

**Starting too far away**
“Since the beginning of time, communication has been important...”

This opening is too broad and difficult to support. Begin closer to the actual research issue.

**Dictionary chain**
“The dictionary defines internet as... The dictionary defines stability as...”

Definitions may be needed later, but a background should explain the research problem, not list dictionary entries.

**Statistic dumping**
Several percentages are placed in one paragraph without explaining how they lead to the local question.

Use fewer, better-explained facts.

**Unsupported local claim**
“Most students in our school have unstable internet.”

Unless authorized local evidence exists, present this as a question to investigate, not as a fact.

**Predetermined conclusion**
“Internet instability is the main reason students submit late.”

This conclusion has been stated before the study. The research should examine the question rather than assume the answer.

**Overpromising**
“The study will eliminate late submission.”

Research may describe or explain a condition. It does not automatically solve it.

**Citation without connection**
A source is summarized, but the writer never explains how it relates to the planned study.

After using evidence, state what it helps establish and what remains unknown.

### 6. Research Questions

A research question states what the study will answer through data collection and analysis. It is not simply a question about the topic. It must be clear, focused, ethical, feasible, and measurable.

Barroga and Matanguihan (2022) explain that quantitative questions typically identify the relevant population and variables and may describe a variable, compare groups, or examine relationships. Saunders et al. (2023) similarly connect the overarching question with research aims, objectives, feasibility, and design.

#### 6.1 The data-answerability test

For every research question, ask:

`What exact data would answer this question?`

If you cannot identify the needed data, the question is probably unclear.

Question:

`What is the level of perceived career readiness among Grade 12 TVL learners after work immersion?`

Possible data:

- responses to a defined and validated or appropriately reviewed career-readiness scale;
- a scoring procedure that produces interpretable levels; and
- data from the population or sample described in the study.

Question:

`Why are students not ready for life?`

The terms “ready” and “life” are not defined, the population is unclear, and a simple quantitative instrument may not answer “why.”

#### 6.2 Descriptive questions

Descriptive questions ask about the status, level, frequency, distribution, profile, or pattern of one or more variables.

Examples:

- What devices do selected Grade 12 learners use for online learning tasks?
- What is the level of perceived career readiness among Grade 12 TVL learners after work immersion?
- How frequently do selected learners use the PR2 reference materials during the stated unit?
- What physical-activity patterns do selected Grade 12 learners report during a regular school week?

Descriptive questions do not test a relationship simply because several characteristics are reported.

#### 6.3 Comparative questions

Comparative questions examine whether an outcome differs across defined groups or conditions.

Pattern:

`Is there a difference in [outcome variable] when respondents are grouped according to [grouping variable]?`

Example:

`Is there a difference in perceived career-readiness scores when Grade 12 TVL learners are grouped according to specialization?`

The study needs:

- a clearly measured outcome;
- meaningful and ethically appropriate groups;
- enough respondents in each group; and
- a suitable statistical procedure.

Do not add a grouping question only because profile variables are available. The comparison should serve the research problem.

#### 6.4 Relationship questions

Relationship questions examine whether two measured variables vary together.

Pattern:

`Is there a relationship between [variable 1] and [variable 2] among [population]?`

Example:

`Is the number of the past four PR2 meetings in which the Unit 4 reference chapter was consulted related to performance on the teacher-approved Unit 4 comprehension task among selected Grade 12 learners?`

A relationship does not automatically mean that one variable causes the other. Other variables may help explain the pattern.

#### 6.5 Causal or intervention questions

Causal questions ask whether an intervention produces a change in an outcome. They require a suitable experimental or quasi-experimental design, clear procedures, ethical approval, comparison conditions when appropriate, and adequate control of alternative explanations.

Example:

`Does the use of a structured title-revision checklist improve title-alignment scores compared with the usual revision procedure among the selected PR2 groups?`

This question cannot be answered by simply asking students whether they liked the checklist.

#### 6.6 Questions that begin with “why”

“Why” questions can be valuable, but they are often difficult for a small quantitative study. Asking why learners submit late may require several interacting variables and possibly qualitative explanations.

Instead of:

`Why do students submit late?`

A focused quantitative study might ask:

- What is the frequency of late submission for the defined tasks?
- What access challenges do learners report?
- Is reported connection stability associated with reported on-time submission?

These questions do not fully explain every cause, but they are more answerable with defined quantitative data.

#### 6.7 Profile questions

Profile variables may include strand, specialization, age group, or another relevant characteristic. Include only information that is necessary for the study.

Do not automatically collect:

- full name;
- exact birth date;
- home address;
- family income;
- medical information;
- account identifiers; or
- other sensitive information.

If a variable does not help answer a research question or describe the sample appropriately, do not collect it. Data minimization protects respondents and keeps the study focused.

### 7. The Main Problem and Specific Questions

The statement of the problem commonly presents one overall problem followed by specific research questions.

The main problem is the umbrella. The specific questions divide that umbrella into answerable parts.

**Main problem:**

`This study aims to determine the relationship between reported internet-connection stability and reported on-time submission of the defined online learning tasks among selected Grade 12 learners during the first semester of School Year 20XX–20XX.`

**Specific questions:**

1. What is the level of reported internet-connection stability among the respondents?
2. What is the frequency of reported on-time submission of the defined online learning tasks?
3. Is there a significant relationship between reported internet-connection stability and reported on-time submission of the defined online learning tasks?

The wording “significant relationship” implies that inferential statistical testing is planned. If the study will only describe a pattern or use a limited sample without an appropriate test, the question should be worded accordingly.

#### 7.1 One question, one clear job

Avoid questions that contain too many tasks.

Overloaded question:

`What are the profile, internet access, study habits, grades, motivation, challenges, solutions, and recommendations of students, and how do all of these affect one another?`

This is a research program, not one manageable question.

Separate only the components required by the study's purpose. Three strong questions are better than ten unrelated ones.

#### 7.2 Do not place recommendations in the opening questions

Students sometimes ask:

`What intervention program may be proposed?`

This can be premature. The study has not yet collected findings, and the PR2 culminating output is the research paper rather than a full intervention program. Recommendations should follow from results and conclusions. Include a program-development question only when the study is actually designed to develop and evaluate such a program.

### 8. Scope, Delimitation, and Limitation

These terms are related but different.

**Scope** states what the study covers.
**Delimitations** state the boundaries deliberately chosen by the researchers.
**Limitations** are constraints or weaknesses that cannot be fully controlled and may affect interpretation.

Some institutional manuals combine these terms or use them differently. Your school template remains important, but the conceptual distinction helps you write more accurately.

#### 8.1 What the scope may identify

The scope may specify:

- the central variables or phenomenon;
- the population or respondents;
- the locale;
- the timeframe;
- the type of data;
- the study design;
- the task, program, subject, or situation covered; and
- the intended level of analysis.

#### 8.2 What delimitations may identify

Delimitations may explain why researchers chose:

- one school rather than an entire division;
- selected Grade 12 sections rather than all grade levels;
- one semester or data-collection window;
- two central variables rather than many possible influences;
- a non-sensitive self-report measure rather than private records;
- a descriptive or correlational design rather than an experiment; or
- one defined task type rather than all schoolwork.

These are deliberate decisions that keep the project ethical and feasible.

#### 8.3 Examples of limitations

Possible limitations include:

- self-report answers may contain recall or social-desirability bias;
- absences or nonresponse may reduce the achieved sample;
- a locally developed instrument may measure the construct imperfectly;
- a one-time survey cannot show changes over time;
- a correlational design cannot establish causation; and
- findings from one school may not apply to other schools.

Limitations are not excuses for careless work. Researchers should reduce avoidable weaknesses through good design, clear instruments, ethical procedures, and honest reporting.

#### 8.4 Model scope and delimitation

> This study will examine the relationship between reported internet-connection stability and reported on-time submission of the online learning tasks assigned in the selected Grade 12 subject during the first semester of School Year 20XX–20XX. The respondents will be selected Grade 12 learners enrolled in the participating sections of the school. Data will be collected using a reviewed questionnaire that defines connection stability and the covered task-submission period.
>
> The study is delimited to one school, the participating Grade 12 sections, the stated semester, and the two central variables. It excludes general academic performance, all causes of late submission, and clinical or household financial information. The study uses a correlational design and will therefore examine association rather than causation.

This model states what is included, what is deliberately excluded, and what kind of conclusion the design can support.

### 9. Writing the Statement of the Problem

The statement of the problem, often shortened to SOP in Philippine research classes, presents the overall research problem and the specific questions that the study will answer.

The SOP should be concise because the background has already explained the context and rationale. It does not need to repeat the entire background.

A practical structure is:

1. a short purpose or main-problem paragraph;
2. a sentence introducing the specific questions; and
3. a numbered list of aligned questions.

#### 9.1 Model for a descriptive study

**Title:**
*Perceived Career Readiness Among Grade 12 TVL Learners After Work Immersion*

**Statement of the problem:**

> This study aims to describe the perceived career readiness of Grade 12 TVL learners who completed the specified work-immersion requirement in the selected school during School Year 20XX–20XX. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following questions:
>
> 1. What is the profile of the respondents according to TVL specialization?
> 2. What is the level of perceived career readiness of the respondents in terms of the dimensions included in the reviewed instrument?
> 3. Which career-readiness dimensions receive the highest and lowest mean ratings?

Question 1 is included because specialization helps describe the respondents and may be relevant to interpreting the results. It should not be included if the study has only one specialization or if it serves no analytical purpose.

#### 9.2 Model for a comparative study

**Title:**
*Perceived Career Readiness Among Grade 12 TVL Learners by Specialization*

**Statement of the problem:**

> This study aims to describe and compare the perceived career readiness of Grade 12 TVL learners who completed the specified work-immersion requirement in the selected school. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following questions:
>
> 1. What is the level of perceived career readiness of the respondents overall and by TVL specialization?
> 2. Is there a significant difference in perceived career-readiness scores when respondents are grouped according to TVL specialization?

This comparative question requires enough respondents in the included specializations and an analysis appropriate to the measurement level and distribution of the data.

#### 9.3 Model for a relationship study

**Title:**
*Use of the Unit 4 PR2 Reference Chapter and Comprehension-Task Performance Among Grade 12 Learners*

**Statement of the problem:**

> This study aims to examine the relationship between the number of the past four relevant PR2 meetings in which the Unit 4 reference chapter was consulted and performance on the teacher-approved 20-item Unit 4 comprehension task among selected Grade 12 learners. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following questions:
>
> 1. In how many of the past four relevant PR2 meetings did the respondents report consulting the Unit 4 reference chapter?
> 2. What is the respondents' performance on the teacher-approved 20-item Unit 4 comprehension task?
> 3. Is there a significant relationship between the number of meetings in which the Unit 4 reference chapter was consulted and Unit 4 comprehension-task performance?

The study does not measure official functional-literacy status. It measures performance on one defined PR2 task.

#### 9.4 Wording “significant” questions carefully

In quantitative research, “significant” often refers to statistical significance. It does not simply mean “important.” Include “significant difference” or “significant relationship” only when:

- the study has an inferential question;
- the sampling and data allow the planned test;
- an appropriate statistical procedure is identified; and
- the teacher has approved the analysis plan.

Statistical significance does not automatically mean that a difference or relationship is large, useful, or educationally important. Practical meaning must still be discussed later.

#### 9.5 SOP problems to avoid

**Question not promised by the title**
The title describes two variables, but the SOP adds satisfaction, motivation, and family income.

**Question with no planned data**
The SOP asks about academic performance, but the instrument collects only opinions about study habits.

**Question outside the approved scope**
The scope covers one school, but the SOP asks about all senior high school learners in the city.

**Question requiring unauthorized records**
The SOP asks for exact grades or attendance records without school approval.

**Causal question with a survey design**
The SOP asks how one variable affects another, but no intervention or causal design exists.

**Recommendation disguised as a research question**
The SOP asks what program should be implemented before findings exist.

### 10. Complete Alignment Models

The following models show how a research idea can remain consistent across the major Unit 2 parts. They are examples for analysis and adaptation, not titles to copy without considering the actual school context.

#### 10.1 Cross-strand model: Digital access and online tasks

**Broad issue:** Learners use different devices and connection arrangements for online tasks.

**Research problem:** The selected school lacks a focused description of reported connection stability and reported on-time submission for the online tasks included in the study.

**Purpose:** Examine whether the two reported variables are associated.

**Variables:**

- reported internet-connection stability;
- reported on-time submission of defined online learning tasks.

**Population:** Selected Grade 12 learners in the participating sections.

**Possible title:**
*Internet-Connection Stability and Reported On-Time Submission of Online Learning Tasks Among Grade 12 Learners*

**Specific questions:**

1. What is the level of reported internet-connection stability among the respondents?
2. What is the reported frequency of on-time submission for the defined online learning tasks?
3. Is there a significant relationship between reported internet-connection stability and reported on-time submission?

**Scope:** One school, selected sections, a specified task type and semester, and two self-reported variables.

**Delimitations:** Does not cover all causes of submission behavior, all academic tasks, exact household income, or overall academic performance.

**Main caution:** A relationship would not prove that connection stability causes submission behavior.

#### 10.2 HUMSS or education model: Reference use and comprehension task

**Broad issue:** Research learning requires learners to understand and apply unfamiliar concepts.

**Research problem:** Use of the Unit 4 PR2 reference chapter may vary, and its relationship with performance on a defined Unit 4 comprehension task is unknown for the selected learners.

**Purpose:** Describe use of the Unit 4 reference chapter and examine its association with Unit 4 task performance.

**Variables:**

- number of the past four relevant PR2 meetings in which the Unit 4 reference chapter was consulted;
- score on the teacher-approved 20-item Unit 4 comprehension task.

**Possible title:**
*Use of the Unit 4 PR2 Reference Chapter and Comprehension-Task Performance Among Grade 12 Learners*

**Specific questions:**

1. In how many of the past four relevant PR2 meetings did respondents consult the Unit 4 reference chapter?
2. What is their performance on the teacher-approved 20-item Unit 4 comprehension task?
3. Is there a significant relationship between the two variables?

**Scope:** The Unit 4 reference chapter, four relevant PR2 meetings, one teacher-approved 20-item Unit 4 task, selected Grade 12 learners, and the stated study period.

**Delimitations:** Does not classify learners' general or functional literacy, measure intelligence, or represent performance in all subjects.

**Main caution:** The study must not label learners through a local task or use national FLEMMS findings as the school's results.

#### 10.3 TVL model: Work immersion and career readiness

**Broad issue:** TVET and work-based learning aim to help learners prepare for career and training pathways.

**Research problem:** Perceived career readiness after work immersion may vary among the selected TVL learners, but the school lacks evidence using a clearly defined measure.

**Purpose:** Describe and, if feasible, compare perceived career readiness.

**Variable:** Perceived career-readiness score.

**Possible grouping variable:** TVL specialization.

**Possible title:**
*Perceived Career Readiness After Work Immersion Among Grade 12 TVL Learners by Specialization*

**Specific questions:**

1. What is the level of perceived career readiness overall and by specialization?
2. Is there a significant difference in perceived career-readiness scores across the included specializations?

**Scope:** Learners who completed the specified work-immersion requirement, selected specializations, one school, and a defined instrument.

**Delimitations:** Does not measure actual employment, employer satisfaction, national certification, or demonstrated occupational competence.

**Main caution:** TESDA graduate outcomes provide public context but cannot be transferred directly to current SHS learners.

#### 10.4 STEM, PE, or health model: Physical activity and classroom alertness

**Broad issue:** Physical activity is one of the behavioral domains included in national health and nutrition monitoring.

**Research problem:** Activity frequency and non-clinical classroom alertness have not been described for the selected Grade 12 learners.

**Purpose:** Describe the variables and examine their relationship.

**Variables:**

- reported physical-activity frequency during a regular school week;
- self-reported classroom alertness using non-clinical items.

**Possible title:**
*Reported Physical-Activity Frequency and Classroom Alertness Among Grade 12 Learners*

**Specific questions:**

1. What physical-activity frequency do respondents report during a regular school week?
2. What is the level of self-reported classroom alertness?
3. Is there a significant relationship between reported physical-activity frequency and classroom alertness?

**Scope:** Non-invasive self-report data, a normal school week, selected learners, and a school-life rather than clinical outcome.

**Delimitations:** Excludes medical diagnosis, weight classification, substance use, mental-health diagnosis, and causal conclusions.

**Main caution:** School approval, parental consent or learner assent as applicable, privacy, and age-appropriate items remain necessary.

#### 10.5 ABM model: Digital payment familiarity and purchase confidence

**Broad issue:** Consumers increasingly encounter digital payment options in commercial settings.

**Research problem:** The selected ABM learners may differ in familiarity with digital payment concepts and confidence in evaluating basic transaction information, but this local condition has not been described.

**Purpose:** Describe the two constructs and examine their relationship.

**Variables:**

- familiarity with defined digital-payment concepts;
- confidence in evaluating basic transaction information.

**Possible title:**
*Digital-Payment Familiarity and Transaction-Information Evaluation Confidence Among Grade 12 ABM Learners*

**Scope:** Knowledge and confidence items approved for classroom research; selected Grade 12 ABM learners; no collection of account numbers, balances, passwords, transaction histories, or personally identifiable financial data.

**Main caution:** Confidence is not the same as actual financial competence. A knowledge or performance task would be needed to measure demonstrated skill.

### 11. Revision as a Research Skill

The first draft is rarely the final version. Revision should improve technical alignment, not only spelling and punctuation.

#### 11.1 Reverse alignment check

Start with the last specific question and work backward.

For each question, identify:

1. the variable or data needed;
2. the planned data source or instrument;
3. the respondents or records involved;
4. the planned type of analysis;
5. the place where the question appears in the background rationale;
6. the title words that represent it; and
7. the scope statement that authorizes it.

If one of these connections is missing, revise before data collection.

#### 11.2 Title-to-question matrix

| Title Element | Where It Appears in Questions | Where It Appears in Scope | Planned Data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internet-connection stability | Questions 1 and 3 | Included variable | Reviewed self-report items |
| Reported on-time submission | Questions 2 and 3 | Defined tasks and period | Reviewed self-report items or authorized task record |
| Grade 12 learners | All questions | Population and participating sections | Approved sample |

A blank cell reveals a likely alignment problem.

#### 11.3 Green, yellow, and red evidence check

**Green: ready to proceed**

- title, questions, and scope use the same central variables;
- the population is consistent;
- each question has a feasible data source;
- causal language matches the design;
- sensitive data have been removed or appropriately safeguarded;
- the background establishes a traceable problem and realistic rationale.

**Yellow: needs targeted revision**

- one term is used inconsistently;
- the title is slightly broader than the questions;
- the local gap needs clearer wording;
- one profile variable lacks a clear purpose;
- the timeframe or task covered is not yet stated.

**Red: blocks the next research step**

- the study promises data it cannot access;
- the questions do not measure the title variables;
- the population changes across sections;
- a survey study makes causal claims;
- the project requires sensitive or identifiable data without approval;
- the proposed study is too broad to complete.

### 12. Ethical Boundaries in Chapter 1

Ethics begins when the problem and questions are chosen, not only when questionnaires are distributed.

#### 12.1 Minimize personal data

Collect only information needed to answer the approved questions. Names are rarely necessary for a simple anonymous survey. Avoid exact identifiers and combinations of profile variables that could reveal a respondent in a small group.

#### 12.2 Avoid stigmatizing labels

Do not frame respondents as lazy, addicted, weak, poor, unhealthy, incompetent, or illiterate. Use neutral, measurable language.

Instead of:

`Why are TVL students unprepared for work?`

Use:

`What is the level of perceived career readiness among the selected Grade 12 TVL learners after work immersion?`

#### 12.3 Do not promise confidentiality casually

The paper should describe actual protections. If group membership or profile combinations make respondents identifiable, calling the data “anonymous” may be inaccurate.

#### 12.4 Sensitive topics require stronger review

Research involving mental health, trauma, substance use, sexual behavior, violence, household income, illegal activity, medical conditions, or other private information may create risk beyond the value of a short Grade 12 project. A safer non-sensitive topic should be selected when adequate safeguards, qualified supervision, and approval are unavailable.

#### 12.5 Public evidence still requires accurate interpretation

Official statistics must be cited with their year, population, definitions, and coverage. Do not remove a percentage from its context or use it to label the local school.

### 13. Common Student Mistakes in Unit 2

#### 13.1 Writing the title before deciding the question

A title can begin as a draft, but it should not be locked until the variables, population, and purpose are clear.

#### 13.2 Treating every topic as causal

Students often prefer “effect,” “impact,” and “influence.” These words sound strong but may make the title technically inaccurate. Use them only when the design supports them.

#### 13.3 Using a national statistic as local proof

National evidence can motivate a local question. It cannot replace local data.

#### 13.4 Writing an overly long background

Length does not guarantee quality. Every paragraph should help establish the context, problem, evidence, gap, rationale, or purpose.

#### 13.5 Inventing a research gap

Do not claim “no studies exist” without a defensible search. State the narrower evidence gap you can support.

#### 13.6 Asking questions the study cannot answer

A one-time survey cannot fully explain every cause, predict the future, or evaluate an intervention that was never implemented.

#### 13.7 Confusing delimitation with limitation

Choosing one school is a delimitation. Nonresponse or self-report bias may be a limitation. Do not present a preventable design error as an unavoidable limitation.

#### 13.8 Adding unnecessary profile variables

“Name, age, sex, strand, religion, income, address, and family background” should not be copied into every questionnaire. Each variable needs a research purpose and ethical justification.

#### 13.9 Changing terms across sections

“Academic performance,” “learning outcome,” “quiz performance,” and “grades” are not automatically interchangeable. Choose the exact variable and use the same term consistently.

#### 13.10 Writing recommendations before results

The background may explain possible usefulness, but recommendations must follow from actual findings later in the paper.

### 14. What a Good Unit 2 Output Looks Like

By the end of Unit 2, a student or research group should have an aligned Chapter 1 foundation.

#### 14.1 Title package

The group should be able to present:

- three defensible title options;
- the purpose represented by each title;
- the central variables or phenomenon;
- the intended population and setting;
- the reason causal or noncausal language was chosen; and
- one revised title selected through an alignment check.

#### 14.2 Background draft

The draft should:

- begin close to the real issue;
- use traceable evidence;
- explain rather than dump statistics;
- narrow from broad context to the specific local question;
- state what remains unknown;
- give a realistic rationale;
- lead clearly to the study purpose; and
- avoid conclusions before data collection.

#### 14.3 Research questions

The questions should:

- use consistent terms;
- identify measurable variables;
- match a descriptive, comparative, relationship, or intervention purpose;
- be answerable through accessible and ethical data;
- fit the available hours and analysis skills; and
- avoid unnecessary questions.

#### 14.4 Scope and delimitation

The section should state:

- what is studied;
- who is included;
- where and when the study applies;
- which variables and data are covered;
- what is deliberately excluded; and
- what level of conclusion is permitted by the design.

#### 14.5 Statement of the problem

The SOP should present:

- one unifying main problem or purpose;
- a concise introduction to the questions;
- logically ordered specific questions; and
- exact alignment with the title and scope.

### 16. Teacher Notes for Unit 2 Delivery

The most important teaching move in Unit 2 is repeated alignment practice. Students should see several mismatched examples before they finalize their own titles.

#### 16.1 Teach with one running example

Carry one example through:

`issue -> problem -> evidence -> gap -> purpose -> questions -> scope -> title`

Then show how one change affects the other parts. If the population changes from all Grade 12 learners to Grade 12 TVL learners, the title, background, questions, scope, sampling plan, and interpretation must all be checked.

#### 16.2 Separate writing quality from research quality

A polished paragraph can still make unsupported claims. During review, check technical content first:

- What is the claim?
- What source supports it?
- Does the source cover this population?
- What remains unknown?
- Does the stated purpose answer that gap?

Grammar editing comes after the logic is sound enough to proceed.

#### 16.3 Use title clinics as decision conferences

Do not judge titles only by length or style. Ask students to explain:

- what each important title term means;
- how it will be measured;
- where the respondents will come from;
- why the wording is descriptive, comparative, relational, or causal; and
- what the title deliberately leaves out.

#### 16.4 Protect students from premature complexity

A smaller aligned study is stronger than an impressive title with many variables, multiple populations, sensitive data, and advanced statistics the group cannot complete.

#### 16.5 Require evidence notes while drafting

For every official statistic or research finding in the background, students should record:

- author or agency;
- publication date;
- exact title;
- URL, DOI, or book details;
- population and location covered;
- the claim the source supports; and
- what the source does not establish.

This habit prepares students for Unit 3 source matrices and RRL writing.

#### 16.6 Use a freeze point at C18

By the end of C18, the Chapter 1 foundation should be marked:

- **Green:** aligned and feasible; proceed to frameworks and RRL;
- **Yellow:** workable but needs a targeted correction that does not block the next step; or
- **Red:** title, questions, scope, ethics, or data access are inconsistent enough to block the next research step.

Only blocking problems should trigger major restructuring. This protects the 80-hour plan from unlimited revisions.

### 17. Final Unit 2 Principle

The title, background, questions, scope, and SOP are different views of the same study.

- The **title** names it.
- The **background** justifies it.
- The **questions** direct it.
- The **scope and delimitations** bound it.
- The **statement of the problem** organizes what it must answer.

If these parts agree, the next units can build a framework, literature review, method, instrument, and analysis plan around a stable foundation. If these parts disagree, later work becomes guesswork.

Before moving forward, be able to state your study in one clear sentence:

`This study will [describe/compare/examine] [the exact variable or variables] among [the exact population] in [the relevant setting and timeframe] using [the feasible type of data].`

That sentence is not the whole research paper. It is evidence that the paper has a coherent beginning.
