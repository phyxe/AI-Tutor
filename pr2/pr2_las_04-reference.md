### Concepts to apply

The exact location of framework sections may vary with the school-approved manuscript format. This stage identifies the required reasoning and evidence, not a mandatory chapter-heading arrangement.

A **theoretical framework** applies a verified established theory as an explanatory lens; a **conceptual framework** organizes the concepts or variables and relationships for *this specific study*. The conceptual framework normally includes both a diagram and an explanation. A **conceptual definition** states what a concept means; an **operational definition** tells how it is represented or measured here. A **hypothesis** is appropriate only when the research question and design justify testing a prediction.

The RRL should follow **MAPS**: **Map** the theme, **Assemble** verified evidence, **Point out** agreement, differences and gaps, and **Show** how the present study fits. A synthesis paragraph may use **TEAL**: **Topic claim → Evidence → Analysis → Link**. These are writing supports, not separate research methods.

### Staged work

1. **Start from the locked research chain.** Read the approved questions, Chapter 3 design, provisional framework, final or pending instrument version and operational measures. List the concepts whose definitions or expected connections require credible support.
2. **Verify the theory decision.** If using a named theory, locate its original or authoritative account, state the relevant proposition, explain why it fits, and acknowledge limits. Do not use an invented or name-only theory. If a specific theory has not been established, record that uncertainty and discuss the appropriate framework with the teacher rather than fabricate one.
3. **Construct and explain the conceptual framework.** Choose a **descriptive dimensions**, **group-comparison**, **relationship**, or genuine **process** structure matching the questions. Name each variable; explain each line or arrow. An association is not a causal effect. Input–Process–Output (IPO) is appropriate only when it represents an actual process being investigated, not simply because the final product is a research paper.
4. **Align operational terms and hypothesis.** Define each central term by meaning, indicator, instrument item/field, unit, coding or scoring rule, and research question. Include a defensible null/alternative hypothesis for an actual difference/relationship test when appropriate; a descriptive study may justify omitting a formal hypothesis.
5. **Build an RRL theme map and verified source matrix.** Organize literature by concepts, measures, relationships and limitations, not merely by author names. For each real source, record author/date, inspected page or section, context/method, relevant result or argument, limitation, theme, and use in the study. An unread abstract does not justify claims about an entire article.
6. **Write synthesized subsections.** Make a theme claim, accurately attribute evidence, compare or qualify sources, and explain how the literature informs a specific question, framework element, instrument choice, hypothesis or local gap. Keep citations matched to full reference entries; avoid patchwriting and fabricated details.
7. **Audit consistency.** Check the framework, definitions and RRL against Chapter 3. If the literature raises a problem with an instrument already locked or used, **do not silently change items or reinterpret previously collected responses**. Refer the inconsistency for an approved decision and document any limitation, revision and effect on comparability.

### Major questions

- What established explanation, concept or prior finding makes each important variable meaningful?
- Exactly what do the boxes and arrows mean, and what evidence supports that interpretation?
- How was each central variable *actually operationalized* in the instrument or dataset?
- Where do the sources agree, disagree, use different measures, or leave a genuine local uncertainty?
- What does this review justify about **our study**, rather than merely describing what previous authors studied?
