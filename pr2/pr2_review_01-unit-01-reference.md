# Unit 1 Reference Chapter: Quantitative Research Foundations and Researchable Problems

## Chapter Overview

Practical Research 2 is about doing quantitative research. This means you will study a problem by using numbers, variables, measurements, and organized procedures. The goal is not only to learn research terms. The goal is to help you write a complete research paper that is clear, feasible, ethical, and useful.

This first unit builds the foundation. You need to know what quantitative research is, what it can do well, what it cannot do well, and how it is used in real situations. You also need to understand variables because variables are the building blocks of a quantitative study. By the end of this unit, you should be able to move from a daily-life issue to a possible research topic with identifiable variables, a possible group of respondents, and a possible source of data.

This chapter is written for students who may be new to research. It starts from basic ideas and slowly builds toward research-paper decisions. If a term feels familiar, still read the examples. In PR2, many errors happen not because students do not know the word, but because they cannot yet use the word correctly in their own study.

The unit uses research-methods ideas from Creswell's research design and educational research books as the main foundation. It also uses current Philippine examples from official and reputable sources, such as DepEd, the Philippine Statistics Authority, the OECD PISA profile for the Philippines, and reputable news and opinion sources. These examples are not meant to become required student topics. They show how everyday concerns can become researchable problems when students learn to identify variables and evidence.

## Key Concepts

| Concept | Plain Meaning |
|---|---|
| Research | A careful and organized way of answering a question or studying a problem |
| Quantitative research | Research that uses numbers, measurement, and statistical analysis to describe, compare, or relate variables |
| Variable | Something that can change, differ, be measured, or be classified |
| Respondents / participants | The people from whom data are collected |
| Population | The larger group that the study is interested in |
| Sample | The smaller group actually included in the study |
| Data | The information collected for the study |
| Instrument | The tool used to collect data, such as a survey questionnaire, checklist, test, or rating scale |
| Descriptive research | Research that describes a condition, trend, level, profile, or practice |
| Correlational research | Research that studies whether two or more variables are related |
| Comparative research | Research that compares groups or categories |
| Experimental research | Research that tests an intervention or treatment under controlled conditions |
| Quasi-experimental research | Research that studies an intervention but does not have full experimental control or random assignment |
| Feasibility | The realistic possibility of completing a study with the available time, respondents, tools, and ethical limits |
| Ethical research | Research that protects participants and uses data honestly |

## Detailed Discussion

### 0. Before Research Terms: What This Subject Is Asking You To Do

Practical Research 2 asks you to build a complete quantitative research paper. This can sound heavy at first, but the paper is built one decision at a time.

You are not expected to know everything on the first day. You are expected to learn how each part of the research paper connects to the next part.

The basic flow is:

`issue -> topic -> variables -> research questions -> framework -> method -> instrument -> data -> results -> conclusions`

If the early parts are clear, the later parts become easier. If the early parts are unclear, the whole paper becomes harder.

For example, if your topic is only "social media," the teacher cannot easily help you write research questions because the topic is too broad. But if your topic is "daily social media use and study time among Grade 12 students," the variables are clearer. You can now ask what data to collect, who to ask, and how to analyze the answers.

This is why Unit 1 focuses on foundations. It teaches you how to think like a beginning quantitative researcher.

### 1. What Research Means in Practical Research 2

Research is a careful way of answering a question. It is different from guessing, reacting, or giving an opinion. A researcher does not simply say, "I think students are distracted by social media." A researcher asks a clearer question, gathers evidence, studies the evidence, and explains what the evidence means.

In Practical Research 2, students are expected to use a quantitative approach. This means they will work with measurable information. They may count responses, compare groups, describe patterns, or study relationships among variables.

For example, you may be interested in the issue of internet access among learners. A casual opinion might say, "Students with weak internet have a hard time studying." A quantitative research version would be more specific:

`What is the relationship between home internet access and completion of online learning tasks among Grade 12 students?`

This version is researchable because it points to variables that can be measured:

- home internet access
- completion of online learning tasks

It also points to a possible group:

- Grade 12 students

The wording is still not a full final title, but it is already closer to a research problem.

Creswell describes research as a process that begins with a problem, moves through literature, purpose, questions, data collection, analysis, and reporting. For Grade 12 students, the important point is simple: research is not one task. It is a chain of connected decisions. A weak decision at the beginning can make the next parts harder. If the topic is unclear, variables become unclear. If variables are unclear, the framework becomes hard to draw. If the framework is weak, the instrument and data analysis may not match the problem.

This is why Unit 1 matters. It sets up the later parts of the paper.

### 2. Quantitative Research in Plain Language

Quantitative research studies a problem by using numbers. These numbers may come from scores, ratings, frequencies, percentages, rankings, counts, or categories that can be analyzed.

In a school setting, quantitative data may include:

- number of hours spent studying
- test scores
- attendance count
- grade average
- number of missed activities
- rating of study habits from 1 to 5
- frequency of social media use
- type of internet access
- strand or section
- level of agreement with survey statements

The word quantitative comes from quantity. A quantity is an amount or number. However, quantitative research is not only about collecting numbers. It is about using numbers to answer a clear research question.

For example:

`How many Grade 12 students have stable internet access at home?`

This is quantitative because it can be answered with numbers, such as frequency and percentage.

`Is there a relationship between daily study time and quiz performance?`

This is also quantitative because both study time and quiz performance can be measured, and the relationship between them can be analyzed.

`What are the experiences of students who struggle with poor internet connection?`

This question is more qualitative because it asks for experiences, stories, meanings, or explanations in the students' own words. It may still be a good research question, but it is not the main focus of Practical Research 2.

Teacher caution: Students often think that adding a survey automatically makes a study quantitative. This is not always true. A survey with mostly open-ended questions may produce qualitative data. A study becomes quantitative when the main data can be counted, measured, classified, compared, or statistically analyzed.

### 3. How Quantitative Research Differs From Qualitative and Mixed Methods Research

Students do not need to become experts in all research approaches in this unit. Still, they need enough contrast to understand what makes their Practical Research 2 paper quantitative.

Quantitative research usually asks questions like:

- How many?
- How much?
- What level?
- What percentage?
- Is there a difference?
- Is there a relationship?
- Which variable predicts or explains another variable?

Qualitative research usually asks questions like:

- What is the experience?
- What meaning do people give to the situation?
- How do people describe the issue?
- What themes appear in interviews or narratives?

Mixed methods research combines quantitative and qualitative data in one study. For example, a researcher may first survey many students and then interview a smaller group to explain the survey results. Mixed methods is useful in advanced research, but it is usually too heavy for a Grade 12 Practical Research 2 paper unless the school specifically requires it.

For PR2, the practical rule is this:

If the main evidence will be numbers, ratings, counts, percentages, scores, or measurable categories, the study is likely quantitative.

### 4. Main Characteristics of Quantitative Research

Quantitative research has several common characteristics. These do not appear in exactly the same way in every study, but they help students recognize and design a quantitative paper.

#### 4.1 It Uses Variables

A variable is something that can change or differ from one person, group, or situation to another. In research, a variable is the thing you observe, measure, compare, or relate to another variable.

Examples:

- study time
- test score
- internet access
- social media use
- reading frequency
- strand
- age
- sex
- attendance
- stress level
- level of agreement

In quantitative research, variables are important because they tell the researcher what to measure.

For example:

`Social media use and academic performance among Grade 12 students`

Possible variables:

- social media use
- academic performance

The first variable may be measured by hours per day, frequency of use, or type of platform used. The second variable may be measured by grade average, quiz scores, or self-reported performance level. The researcher must decide how each variable will be measured. That later decision is called operational definition, which will be discussed more deeply in Unit 3.

#### 4.2 It Uses Measurement

Quantitative research needs measurement. To measure means to assign a number, score, category, or level to something in a consistent way.

For example, "internet access" is a broad idea. To measure it, a researcher may ask:

- Does the learner have internet access at home? Yes or no.
- What type of connection is used? Mobile data, wired broadband, prepaid Wi-Fi, school Wi-Fi, or none.
- How many hours per day can the learner access the internet?
- How stable is the connection on a scale from 1 to 5?

Each choice gives a different kind of data. Students must learn that a variable is not enough. They must also ask, "How will I measure this variable?"

#### 4.3 It Uses Structured Tools

Quantitative research usually uses structured tools. These tools collect the same kind of information from each respondent. Examples include:

- survey questionnaire
- checklist
- test
- rating scale
- observation form
- data sheet

Structured tools help the researcher compare answers. If one student answers a question about study time and another student answers a totally different question, the results will be difficult to analyze. A common set of questions makes the data more organized.

#### 4.4 It Uses a Sample

Many studies cannot include every person in the population. A population is the larger group the study is interested in. A sample is the smaller group that actually joins the study.

For example:

- Population: all Grade 12 students in the school
- Sample: 80 Grade 12 students who answered the survey

The sample must be chosen carefully. If the sample is too small, too biased, or too hard to reach, the results may be weak. Sampling is discussed more deeply in Unit 4, but you should already understand that the research topic must involve a reachable group of respondents.

#### 4.5 It Uses Statistical Analysis

Statistics help researchers summarize and study numerical data. In Grade 12 PR2, the statistics should be kept manageable. Students may use:

- frequency
- percentage
- mean
- ranking
- simple comparison
- simple relationship analysis, when appropriate

Do not use a statistical test just to look advanced. The analysis must match the research question and the type of data.

For example, if the question asks about the most common source of internet access, frequency and percentage may be enough. If the question asks whether study time is related to quiz score, a relationship-based analysis may be needed.

#### 4.6 It Tries to Be Objective

Objectivity means the researcher tries to reduce personal bias. This does not mean the researcher has no opinions. It means the researcher does not let opinions replace evidence.

For example, you may believe that mobile games reduce study time. That belief may inspire the topic, but the study still needs evidence. As the researcher, you must collect data honestly and report the findings even if the results do not match your original belief.

Teacher caution: Students may enter PR2 with a topic they already feel strongly about. That is acceptable as a starting point, but they must learn to convert strong opinions into researchable questions.

### 5. Strengths of Quantitative Research

Quantitative research is useful because it can make patterns visible. It can help teachers, students, school leaders, and communities understand what is happening across a group.

#### 5.1 It Can Describe a Group Clearly

Quantitative research can show how common something is.

Example:

`What percentage of Grade 12 students use mobile data as their main internet source?`

This kind of study can produce a clear result, such as:

`Sixty percent of the respondents reported using mobile data as their main internet source.`

The result is easy to summarize and compare.

In the Philippine context, the Philippine Statistics Authority reported that 48.8 percent of households had internet access at home in 2024, while 67.3 percent of individuals aged 10 and over reported using the internet at any location. These national numbers do not automatically describe one school, but they show how quantitative data can describe access and usage at a large scale. A student study can use the same idea at a smaller school level.

#### 5.2 It Can Compare Groups

Quantitative research can compare groups when the comparison is fair and ethical.

Examples:

- study habits of HUMSS and ABM students
- average sleep hours of students with morning classes and afternoon classes
- perceived research anxiety of students with and without prior research experience

Comparisons should not be used to shame groups. They should help understand differences that may need support.

#### 5.3 It Can Study Relationships

Quantitative research can study whether two variables move together.

Example:

`Is there a relationship between daily study time and quiz performance?`

If students who study longer tend to have higher quiz scores, the variables may be related. But relationship does not automatically mean cause. Maybe students who study longer also have quieter homes, better internet access, or stronger prior knowledge. These other factors may affect the result.

Teacher caution: This is one of the most important lessons in Unit 1. Correlation does not prove causation. A relationship between variables does not automatically mean that one variable caused the other.

#### 5.4 It Can Support Decisions

Quantitative research can support decisions because it gives evidence. In schools, research may help teachers or student leaders decide what programs, materials, or interventions may be needed.

Example:

If many students report difficulty completing online tasks because of unstable internet, the school may consider printed alternatives, offline file distribution, or adjusted submission procedures. The student research does not make the decision by itself, but it can provide evidence for discussion.

This connects with DepEd's learning continuity concerns. DepEd has emphasized the need for Alternative Delivery Modes during heat, disasters, and other emergencies. It also reported that some regions experienced many class disruptions due to natural hazards. These situations show why schools need local evidence about learners' access, readiness, and support systems.

### 6. Weaknesses and Limits of Quantitative Research

Quantitative research is powerful, but it is not perfect. You need to know its limits before you design a study.

#### 6.1 It May Not Explain the Whole Story

Numbers can show what is happening, but they may not fully explain why it is happening.

Example:

A survey may show that 70 percent of respondents prefer printed materials during class suspensions. But the number alone may not explain all the reasons. Some students may prefer print because of weak internet. Others may prefer it because they share one phone with siblings. Others may simply find printed tasks easier to focus on.

Quantitative research can include some explanation through carefully designed survey items, but deep personal stories are usually better studied through qualitative research.

#### 6.2 It Depends on Good Measurement

A weak instrument can produce weak data.

Example:

If a survey asks, "Do you always study responsibly?" many students may answer yes because the question sounds judgmental. The result may not be useful.

A better item might ask:

`How many hours did you spend reviewing lessons yesterday?`

or

`How often do you review your notes before a quiz?`

Good measurement is specific, fair, and understandable.

#### 6.3 It Can Hide Context

A number can summarize a situation, but it can also hide details.

Example:

Two students may both report "2 hours of internet use for schoolwork." But one may have stable Wi-Fi and a laptop, while the other may use borrowed mobile data on a shared phone. The number looks the same, but the learning situation is different.

This does not mean quantitative research is wrong. It means the researcher must be careful when interpreting data.

#### 6.4 It Can Be Affected by Sampling Problems

If the sample is not appropriate, the result may not represent the group well.

Example:

If a study about internet access only surveys students who are active in an online group chat, it may miss students with weaker internet access. The result may make internet access look better than it really is.

This is why sampling matters.

#### 6.5 It Can Be Misused

Numbers can look convincing even when the research is weak. A table or graph can make a claim appear scientific, but the claim may still be wrong if the data were poorly collected or poorly interpreted.

Students must learn to ask:

- Where did the data come from?
- Who answered?
- How were the questions worded?
- What was actually measured?
- What does the result prove?
- What does the result not prove?

This is part of ethical research thinking.

### 7. Kinds of Quantitative Research

Different kinds of quantitative research answer different kinds of questions. In Unit 1, students only need an introductory understanding. The deeper decision about research design happens in Unit 4.

#### 7.1 Descriptive Research

Descriptive research describes what exists. It may describe a profile, level, frequency, trend, practice, or condition.

Common question forms:

- What is the level of ______?
- What is the profile of ______?
- How often do students ______?
- What percentage of respondents ______?

Example:

`What is the level of research anxiety among Grade 12 students?`

Possible variables:

- research anxiety level

Possible data:

- rating scale responses

Possible result:

- low, moderate, or high level of research anxiety

Descriptive research is often realistic for Grade 12 because it does not require the researcher to prove cause and effect.

Philippine-context example:

The PSA's 2024 ICT survey reported national and regional patterns in internet access and use. A Grade 12 descriptive study could use the same general idea at school level by describing students' main internet source, average daily school-related internet use, or access to devices.

#### 7.2 Survey Research

Survey research gathers information from respondents by asking the same questions in a structured way. Survey research can be descriptive, correlational, or comparative depending on the research questions.

Example:

`A survey on the study habits and learning resource preferences of Grade 12 students`

A survey can collect data from many respondents in a short time. However, the quality of the survey depends on the quality of the questions. Confusing, biased, or double-barreled questions can damage the study.

Double-barreled question:

`Do you agree that online learning is convenient and effective?`

This is weak because convenient and effective are two different ideas. You may find online learning convenient but not effective, or effective but not convenient.

Better version:

`Online learning is convenient for me.`

`Online learning helps me understand the lesson.`

Each item measures one idea.

#### 7.3 Correlational Research

Correlational research studies whether two or more variables are related.

Common question forms:

- Is there a relationship between ______ and ______?
- To what extent is ______ related to ______?

Example:

`Is there a relationship between study time and quiz performance among Grade 12 students?`

Possible variables:

- study time
- quiz performance

Possible data:

- number of study hours
- quiz scores

Important caution:

Correlation does not prove cause. If study time and quiz performance are related, the researcher still cannot automatically claim that study time caused the quiz score. Other factors may be involved.

This kind of design requires careful teacher guidance because it is easy to overclaim.

#### 7.4 Comparative Research

Comparative research compares two or more groups.

Common question forms:

- Is there a significant difference between ______ and ______?
- How do groups differ in terms of ______?

Example:

`Is there a difference in research anxiety between students with prior research experience and those without prior research experience?`

Possible grouping variable:

- prior research experience: yes or no

Possible outcome variable:

- research anxiety level

Comparative research is useful when groups are natural or already existing. However, comparisons must be handled carefully. Avoid topics that shame, stereotype, or unfairly label groups.

#### 7.5 Experimental Research

Experimental research tests whether an intervention or treatment affects an outcome. In a true experiment, the researcher has strong control, usually including random assignment.

Example:

`The effect of a vocabulary review strategy on quiz performance`

Possible independent variable:

- review strategy

Possible dependent variable:

- quiz performance

True experimental research is often difficult in Grade 12 school settings. It may require permissions, controlled groups, time, and ethical safeguards. Do not choose an experiment just because it sounds impressive.

#### 7.6 Quasi-Experimental Research

Quasi-experimental research also studies an intervention, but the researcher does not have full experimental control. For example, existing sections may be compared instead of randomly assigning students.

Example:

`The effect of a guided review sheet on the quiz scores of one Grade 12 section`

This may be more realistic than a true experiment, but it still requires careful planning. Students must ask:

- Is the intervention allowed?
- Who will be affected?
- Is there enough time?
- How will fairness be protected?
- What data will show the result?

Teacher caution: In many Grade 12 PR2 classes, descriptive, correlational, or simple comparative studies are more feasible than experimental or quasi-experimental studies. Unit 4 will help students choose the best design.

### 8. Why Quantitative Research Matters Across Fields

Quantitative research is not only for scientists or statisticians. It is used in education, business, health, technology, public policy, sports, communication, and community development.

#### 8.1 Education

Schools use quantitative data to understand attendance, grades, test performance, resource needs, and program outcomes.

Example topics:

- study habits and academic performance
- attendance and completion of performance tasks
- internet access and submission of learning outputs
- preferred learning resources during class suspensions
- research anxiety and manuscript progress

The Philippines' PISA 2022 results provide a national example of how quantitative assessment data can describe education outcomes. OECD reported the Philippines' mean scores in mathematics, reading, and science in PISA 2022. These results do not tell the whole story of every learner, but they help show why education systems use large-scale quantitative data to monitor learning and identify gaps.

For a Grade 12 PR2 class, the lesson is not to copy PISA. The lesson is that education problems often need measurable evidence.

#### 8.2 Business and ABM Contexts

Business decisions often use quantitative data. Entrepreneurs and managers may count sales, compare customer ratings, study buying behavior, or measure satisfaction.

Example topics:

- buying preferences of students for school snacks
- factors related to student budgeting practices
- customer satisfaction with a school-based business project
- relationship between product price and purchase intention

ABM students can use quantitative research to study consumer behavior, financial habits, service quality, and entrepreneurship practices.

#### 8.3 HUMSS and Social Issues

HUMSS students may study social behavior, communication, civic participation, media use, school climate, or learner attitudes.

Example topics:

- social media exposure and news verification habits
- student participation in school organizations
- attitudes toward community service
- perceived safety and school attendance
- media literacy and sharing behavior

The PSA's 2024 FLEMMS report stated that 80.5 percent of Filipinos aged 10 to 64 used the internet for social media. This national figure can help explain why topics about social media, media literacy, or online behavior matter. However, you still need local data if your study is about students in a specific school.

#### 8.4 STEM Contexts

STEM students may study measurable relationships, environmental conditions, health habits, or technology use.

Example topics:

- sleep hours and reaction time
- screen time and eye strain
- study time and mathematics quiz performance
- classroom temperature and perceived concentration
- device access and completion of digital activities

Design thinking may be useful for STEM learners, but it should be used lightly in PR2. It can help students notice real problems and define them clearly. It should not turn the paper into a product-design project unless that is required by another subject.

#### 8.5 TVL and Work-Related Contexts

TVL students may study skills, workplace readiness, safety practices, tool use, service quality, or customer satisfaction.

Example topics:

- workplace readiness of TVL students
- safety practices during laboratory activities
- relationship between practice hours and skills assessment scores
- customer satisfaction with student-led services
- tool availability and task completion

Quantitative research can help TVL students connect classroom training with measurable performance and workplace expectations.

### 9. Variables: The Building Blocks of Quantitative Research

Variables are central to quantitative research. If students do not understand variables, they will struggle with the title, research questions, framework, instrument, and data analysis.

#### 9.1 Variable in Plain Language

A variable is something that can vary. It is not the same for everyone.

Examples:

- age varies
- study time varies
- score varies
- internet access varies
- strand varies
- level of agreement varies
- number of absences varies

If something does not change in the study, it is not acting as a variable.

Example:

If all respondents are Grade 12 HUMSS students, then "grade level" is not a variable in that study because it does not vary. It is part of the scope.

#### 9.2 Quantitative and Categorical Variables

Some variables are measured with numbers.

Examples:

- test score
- number of study hours
- age
- number of absences
- rating from 1 to 5

Some variables are categories.

Examples:

- strand: HUMSS, ABM, STEM, TVL, GAS
- sex: male, female, prefer not to say, or categories approved by the school
- main internet source: mobile data, wired broadband, school Wi-Fi, none
- type of device: smartphone, laptop, tablet, desktop

Categorical variables can still be used in quantitative research because they can be counted and compared.

#### 9.3 Independent and Dependent Variables

An independent variable is the variable that may explain, influence, predict, or group another variable.

A dependent variable is the variable being explained, influenced, predicted, or compared.

Example:

`Relationship between study time and quiz performance`

Possible independent variable:

- study time

Possible dependent variable:

- quiz performance

This does not automatically prove that study time causes quiz performance. It only shows how the variables are positioned in the study.

Teacher caution: In descriptive studies, students may not always need independent and dependent variables. Sometimes they only describe one main variable, such as research anxiety level or internet access profile. Do not force every descriptive study into an independent-dependent pattern.

#### 9.4 Profile Variables

Profile variables describe the respondents.

Examples:

- age
- sex
- strand
- section
- type of device used
- internet access type

Profile variables can help describe the sample. They can also be used for comparison if the research questions require it.

Example:

`Is there a difference in research anxiety when students are grouped by strand?`

Here, strand is a grouping variable.

#### 9.5 Control and Extraneous Variables

A control variable is a variable the researcher tries to keep the same or account for.

An extraneous variable is another factor that may affect the result but is not the main focus of the study.

Example:

A study looks at the relationship between study time and quiz performance. Other factors may affect quiz performance, such as prior knowledge, sleep, health, teacher support, or home responsibilities. These are possible extraneous variables.

Grade 12 students do not need to control every factor perfectly. But they should acknowledge that research has limits. They should not claim more than their data can support.

#### 9.6 Constructs and Variables

Some research ideas are easy to measure. For example, age, number of absences, and quiz score are direct. You can ask, count, or record them.

Other ideas are broader. Examples include stress, motivation, satisfaction, confidence, anxiety, readiness, and study habits. These are not directly visible like age or score. In research, these broad ideas are often called constructs.

A construct is an idea that cannot be observed directly but can be studied through indicators.

For example:

`research confidence`

You cannot open a notebook and see "confidence" directly. But you can ask indicators such as:

- I can choose a research topic.
- I can identify variables in a study.
- I can write research questions.
- I can revise my work after feedback.

If students answer these items using a scale, the researcher can measure research confidence through those indicators.

This is important because many Grade 12 topics use constructs. You may want to study "stress," "motivation," or "satisfaction." These can become quantitative variables, but only if you decide how to measure them.

Teacher caution: A broad construct should not be placed directly in a title unless the class knows how it will be measured. For example, "stress" should become clearer through a scale, rating, checklist, or specific indicators.

#### 9.7 Indicators

An indicator is a visible or measurable sign of a variable or construct.

Example:

Construct:

`study habits`

Possible indicators:

- number of hours spent reviewing lessons
- frequency of note-taking
- frequency of reviewing before quizzes
- use of a study schedule
- completion of assigned tasks

Construct:

`internet access`

Possible indicators:

- presence of internet at home
- type of internet connection
- daily hours of access
- stability rating
- device used for school tasks

Construct:

`research anxiety`

Possible indicators:

- worry when choosing a topic
- fear of writing the research paper
- difficulty asking for feedback
- nervousness about data analysis
- avoidance of research tasks

Indicators help students move from a vague topic to measurable data. If a student cannot name indicators, the variable may still be too unclear.

#### 9.8 Independent and Dependent Variables Are About the Role in the Study

You may ask, "Is this variable always independent?" or "Is this variable always dependent?" The answer is no.

The role of a variable depends on the study.

Example 1:

`Relationship between study time and quiz performance`

- study time may be the independent variable
- quiz performance may be the dependent variable

Example 2:

`Difference in study time when students are grouped by internet access`

- internet access may be the grouping or independent variable
- study time may be the dependent variable

Example 3:

`Relationship between research confidence and study time`

- research confidence and study time may simply be related variables
- the study may not claim that one causes the other

This is why you should not memorize variable roles without looking at the research question.

Decision rule:

Ask, "What is being used to explain, group, compare, or predict?" That variable often acts like the independent variable.

Ask, "What is being described, compared, explained, predicted, or measured as the result?" That variable often acts like the dependent variable.

#### 9.9 Predictor and Outcome Variables

Sometimes the words independent and dependent sound too causal. They may make students think that one variable causes another. For non-experimental studies, safer words may be predictor and outcome.

A predictor variable is used to predict or explain differences in another variable.

An outcome variable is the result or condition being studied.

Example:

`Study time as a predictor of quiz performance`

- predictor variable: study time
- outcome variable: quiz performance

This wording is useful when the study does not prove cause and effect.

#### 9.10 Grouping Variables

A grouping variable places respondents into categories.

Examples:

- strand: HUMSS, ABM, STEM, TVL, GAS
- sex
- section
- type of internet access
- prior research experience: yes or no
- main device used: phone, laptop, tablet, desktop

Grouping variables are common in comparative research.

Example:

`Difference in research confidence when students are grouped by prior research experience`

Grouping variable:

- prior research experience

Outcome variable:

- research confidence

Teacher caution: Grouping variables can lead to unfair or sensitive comparisons. A study comparing groups should have a good reason and should avoid shaming or stereotyping.

#### 9.11 Quantitative Variables and Categorical Variables

A quantitative variable uses numbers that show amount, score, frequency, or level.

Examples:

- age
- number of hours online
- quiz score
- number of absences
- rating from 1 to 5

A categorical variable uses labels or groups.

Examples:

- strand
- sex
- type of device
- main internet source
- preferred learning resource

Both can be used in quantitative research. The difference is the kind of data they give.

Example:

`Device used for school tasks`

This is categorical because the answers may be smartphone, laptop, tablet, or desktop.

`Number of hours spent using a device for school tasks`

This is quantitative because the answers are amounts of time.

#### 9.12 Discrete and Continuous Variables

Some numerical variables are discrete. This means they are counted in whole numbers.

Examples:

- number of siblings
- number of absences
- number of completed tasks
- number of books read

Some numerical variables are continuous. This means they can be measured with many possible values, including decimals.

Examples:

- time spent studying
- height
- weight
- average grade
- temperature

For Grade 12 research, students do not need to overcomplicate this. The main use is to understand how the variable can be measured and summarized.

#### 9.13 Levels of Measurement

Levels of measurement explain what kind of numbers or categories a variable uses. This matters later because the type of data affects the analysis.

There are four common levels:

| Level | Plain Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal | categories with no order | strand, device type, internet source |
| Ordinal | categories with order | low/moderate/high, rank, satisfaction level |
| Interval | numbers with equal spaces but no true zero | temperature in Celsius |
| Ratio | numbers with equal spaces and true zero | age, study hours, number of absences |

For Grade 12 PR2, the most common are nominal, ordinal, and ratio.

Example:

`Preferred learning material`

Possible answers:

- printed module
- PDF file
- video lesson
- live online class

This is nominal because the categories have no natural order.

Example:

`Agreement with the statement: I can study independently during class suspensions.`

Possible answers:

- strongly disagree
- disagree
- agree
- strongly agree

This is ordinal because the answers have order.

Example:

`Number of hours spent studying yesterday`

This is ratio because zero means no study time, and the numbers show real amounts.

Teacher caution: Many survey scales use numbers, such as 1 to 5. Students may think these are automatically ratio data because they are numbers. In many classroom studies, rating scales are treated carefully because the numbers represent ordered responses. The teacher should guide which analysis is appropriate.

#### 9.14 Confounding Variables

A confounding variable is an outside factor that may affect the relationship between the variables in the study.

Example:

A student study asks:

`Is there a relationship between study time and quiz performance?`

Possible confounding variables:

- prior knowledge
- sleep
- access to learning materials
- home responsibilities
- health
- language difficulty

If students with more study time also have better access to learning materials, then access to materials may affect the result. The study may still be useful, but the researcher should not claim too much.

In Grade 12, students do not need to solve every confounding problem. But they should know that other factors can affect results. This helps them write limitations honestly.

#### 9.15 Moderator and Mediator Variables

These terms are more advanced, but students may encounter them in research articles.

A moderator variable changes the strength or direction of a relationship.

Example:

Study time may be related to quiz performance, but the relationship may be stronger for students with quiet study spaces than for students without quiet study spaces. In this example, quiet study space may act like a moderator.

A mediator variable helps explain how or why one variable is related to another.

Example:

Internet access may be related to task completion because students with better access can download materials more easily. In this example, access to materials may help explain the relationship.

For most Grade 12 PR2 studies, students do not need to design studies with moderators or mediators. But knowing these terms helps them understand that variable relationships can be complex.

#### 9.16 How to Identify Variables in a Title

A useful way to identify variables is to look for what can change, be measured, be grouped, or be compared.

Example title:

`Relationship between daily social media use and study time among Grade 12 students`

Step 1: Remove the respondents.

`among Grade 12 students`

This tells who the study is about. It is not the main variable unless the study compares grade levels.

Step 2: Look for the measurable ideas.

- daily social media use
- study time

Step 3: Ask how each can be measured.

- daily social media use: hours per day, frequency, platform use
- study time: hours per day, days per week, review frequency

Step 4: Ask the relationship or action.

The title says "relationship between," so the study will likely examine whether the two variables are related.

Example title:

`Research confidence of Grade 12 students by strand`

Respondents:

- Grade 12 students

Main variable:

- research confidence

Grouping variable:

- strand

Possible design:

- descriptive-comparative

Example title:

`Preferred learning resources during class suspensions among Grade 12 students`

Respondents:

- Grade 12 students

Main variable:

- preferred learning resources

Possible related profile variables:

- internet access
- device access
- type of material preferred

Possible design:

- descriptive survey

#### 9.17 How to Identify Variables in a Research Question

Research questions often show the variables more clearly than titles.

Question:

`What is the level of research anxiety among Grade 12 students?`

Variable:

- research anxiety level

Question:

`Is there a significant relationship between study time and quiz performance?`

Variables:

- study time
- quiz performance

Question:

`Is there a significant difference in research confidence when students are grouped according to strand?`

Grouping variable:

- strand

Outcome variable:

- research confidence

Question:

`What learning resources do students prefer during class suspensions?`

Variable:

- preferred learning resources

Possible categories:

- printed module
- PDF file
- recorded video
- live online class
- teacher-made notes

#### 9.18 Variable Identification Table

Students can use this table to test whether their variables are clear.

| Question | What to Write |
|---|---|
| What is the variable? | Name the idea clearly |
| What type of variable is it? | Quantitative, categorical, grouping, predictor, outcome, profile |
| How can it change? | Explain how answers may differ |
| How can it be measured? | Survey item, scale, score, count, record, checklist |
| Who will provide the data? | Respondents, teacher records if allowed, observation |
| Is it ethical to collect? | Yes/no, and why |
| Is it feasible? | Can it be collected within time and school rules? |

Example:

| Question | Example Answer |
|---|---|
| What is the variable? | Study time |
| What type of variable is it? | Quantitative predictor variable |
| How can it change? | Some students study longer than others |
| How can it be measured? | Number of hours spent studying per day |
| Who will provide the data? | Student respondents |
| Is it ethical to collect? | Yes, if no names are required |
| Is it feasible? | Yes, through a short survey |

#### 9.19 Common Errors in Identifying Variables

Error 1:

`My variable is Grade 12 students.`

Correction:

Grade 12 students are the respondents. They are not the variable unless the study compares grade levels.

Error 2:

`My variable is social media.`

Correction:

Social media is too broad. Possible variables are daily time spent on social media, type of platform used, purpose of use, or frequency of use.

Error 3:

`My variable is effect.`

Correction:

Effect is not usually the variable. Ask: effect on what? Test score? Study time? Attendance? Confidence?

Error 4:

`My variables are students and performance.`

Correction:

Students are the respondents. Performance must be made specific, such as quiz score, average grade, task completion rate, or self-rated academic performance.

Error 5:

`My variable is online learning.`

Correction:

Online learning is broad. Possible variables include frequency of online class attendance, perceived effectiveness of online learning, access to online materials, or difficulty completing online tasks.

#### 9.20 Why Variables Matter for the Whole Paper

Variables are not only for Unit 1. They appear in almost every part of the research paper.

| Paper Part | How Variables Are Used |
|---|---|
| Title | Names or implies the main variables |
| Background | Explains why the variables matter |
| Research questions | Ask about the variables |
| Scope and delimitation | Limits which variables will be studied |
| Conceptual framework | Shows how variables may relate |
| Hypothesis | Predicts a relationship or difference between variables, if appropriate |
| Definition of terms | Explains how variables are understood and measured |
| Instrument | Collects data about the variables |
| Data analysis | Summarizes, compares, or relates variables |
| Conclusions | Answer questions about the variables |

This is why variable identification is one of the most important skills in Practical Research 2.

### 10. From Daily-Life Issue to Researchable Problem

Many students start with broad topics:

- social media
- stress
- internet
- study habits
- bullying
- attendance
- reading
- climate change
- work immersion
- school facilities

These are not yet research problems. They are areas of interest. A research problem must be narrower, measurable, and realistic.

#### 10.1 The Problem-Refinement Path

Students can use this path:

1. Notice the issue
2. Define the specific problem
3. Identify who is affected
4. Identify what can be measured
5. Decide what kind of question can be answered
6. Check if the study is feasible

This is inspired by design thinking, but adapted for PR2. Design thinking often begins with understanding people and contexts, then defining the problem, generating ideas, making early versions, and testing or improving them. For PR2, the purpose is not to build a product. The purpose is to refine a real concern into a researchable problem.

#### 10.2 Example: From Broad Topic to Researchable Topic

Broad topic:

`Internet access`

More specific issue:

`Some students may have difficulty completing online learning tasks because of unstable internet access.`

Possible research topic:

`Home internet access and completion of online learning tasks among Grade 12 students`

Possible variables:

- home internet access
- completion of online learning tasks

Possible respondents:

- Grade 12 students

Possible data source:

- survey questionnaire
- class submission records, if allowed and anonymized

Possible design:

- descriptive or correlational

Feasibility check:

- Can students answer honestly?
- Can the researcher collect data without exposing private information?
- Is the topic allowed by the school?
- Can it be completed within the term?

#### 10.3 Example: From News Issue to School Research Topic

Context:

DepEd has reported class disruptions due to storms and other hazards, and has emphasized learning continuity through Alternative Delivery Modes. This national issue can inspire local school questions.

Broad topic:

`Class suspensions`

Possible research topic:

`Learning resource preferences during class suspensions among Grade 12 students`

Possible variables:

- preferred learning resource
- access to device
- internet access
- perceived difficulty of completing tasks

Possible design:

- descriptive survey

Teacher caution:

Students must avoid making claims about the whole Philippines if they only surveyed one section or one school. The national source provides context, not local proof.

### 11. Feasibility: The First Gate of Student Research

A topic may be interesting but still not feasible.

Feasibility means the study can be completed with the time, skills, respondents, tools, and permissions available.

#### 11.1 Feasibility Questions

Ask these questions:

- Can this topic be studied using numbers?
- Are the variables clear?
- Can the variables be measured with a survey, checklist, score, rating, or record?
- Are the respondents reachable?
- Is the topic safe and ethical?
- Can the study be completed within the term?
- Is the topic too sensitive?
- Does the school allow this kind of data collection?
- Will the results help explain a real issue?

#### 11.2 Topics That May Be Too Broad

Too broad:

`The effect of social media on students`

Better:

`Relationship between daily social media use and self-reported study time among Grade 12 students`

Too broad:

`The impact of poverty on education`

Better:

`Relationship between availability of learning resources at home and completion of written tasks among Grade 12 students`

Too broad:

`Climate change and education`

Better:

`Preferred learning modality during heat-related class suspensions among Grade 12 students`

#### 11.3 Topics That May Be Too Sensitive

Be careful with topics involving:

- mental health diagnosis
- family income
- abuse
- illegal behavior
- sexual behavior
- medical conditions
- political preference
- religious conflict
- teacher performance
- personally identifiable school records

Some sensitive topics can be studied by trained researchers with proper ethics review. But for Grade 12 PR2, the safer route is to choose topics that protect respondents and can be answered without harm.

Example:

Instead of asking students to report family income, the researcher may study access to learning resources. Instead of asking for diagnosed anxiety, the researcher may study self-reported research confidence or perceived difficulty.

### 12. Philippine Context Examples for Topic Generation

The following examples show how official data or reputable news can help students think of researchable topics. These examples are not automatic research titles. They are starting points for local topic refinement.

#### 12.1 Internet Access and Learning Tasks

The PSA reported that 48.8 percent of Philippine households had internet access at home in 2024. It also reported that 67.3 percent of individuals aged 10 and over had used the internet at any location. These figures show that internet use is common, but home access is not universal.

Possible school-level research ideas:

- internet access and completion of online tasks
- type of device used and perceived ease of completing digital outputs
- average school-related internet use and submission punctuality
- preferred learning resource during class suspensions

Possible variables:

- home internet access
- device used
- internet stability rating
- completion of tasks
- submission punctuality
- preferred learning resource

Why this matters:

This topic connects to real school conditions. It can help a teacher understand whether learners can reasonably complete online or blended tasks.

#### 12.2 Social Media Use and Study Behavior

The PSA's 2024 FLEMMS report stated that 80.5 percent of Filipinos aged 10 to 64 used the internet for social media. This does not prove that social media harms or helps students. It only shows that social media use is widespread.

Possible school-level research ideas:

- daily social media use and self-reported study time
- social media use and sleep hours
- social media as source of school announcements
- social media exposure and news verification habits

Possible variables:

- daily time spent on social media
- study time
- sleep hours
- frequency of checking school announcements
- news verification habits

Teacher caution:

Students must avoid moral panic. A study should not begin with the assumption that social media is automatically bad. The research should ask a fair and measurable question.

#### 12.3 Learning Disruptions and Alternative Delivery Modes

DepEd has issued guidance on class suspensions and shifts to Alternative Delivery Modes during extreme heat, disasters, and other emergencies. DepEd also reported class disruptions in regions affected by natural hazards.

Possible school-level research ideas:

- learner readiness for independent work during class suspensions
- preferred format of self-paced learning materials
- relationship between access to printed materials and task completion
- perceived difficulty of modular tasks during weather-related suspensions

Possible variables:

- readiness for independent learning
- access to printed materials
- task completion
- preferred learning format
- perceived difficulty

Why this matters:

This connects directly to the Learning Module design in the PR2 curriculum plan. It also helps students see that research can support learning continuity.

#### 12.4 Learning Outcomes and Study Support

The OECD PISA 2022 profile for the Philippines reported mean scores for Filipino 15-year-old students in mathematics, reading, and science. These large-scale results are not the same as a local school study, but they show how quantitative data can be used to monitor learning outcomes.

Possible school-level research ideas:

- study habits and quiz performance
- reading frequency and comprehension test scores
- confidence in mathematics and performance in problem-solving tasks
- use of review materials and short test performance

Possible variables:

- study habits
- reading frequency
- academic confidence
- review material use
- quiz or test performance

Teacher caution:

Do not use PISA results to label your classmates. PISA is a national and international assessment context. In PR2, it is better used as background for why learning outcomes and support systems are important.

### 13. Common Student Mistakes in Unit 1

When you begin quantitative research, some mistakes are very common. It is better to catch them early.

#### 13.1 Mistaking a Topic for a Research Problem

`Social media` is a topic, not yet a research problem.

Better:

`Relationship between daily social media use and study time among Grade 12 students`

#### 13.2 Choosing Variables That Cannot Be Measured

Weak variable:

`student success`

Better variables:

- grade average
- completion rate of tasks
- quiz score
- self-rated confidence
- attendance

#### 13.3 Making the Topic Too Big

Weak:

`The impact of technology on Philippine education`

Better:

`Relationship between device access and completion of digital learning tasks among Grade 12 students in [school]`

#### 13.4 Making Causal Claims Too Early

Weak:

`Social media causes low grades.`

Better:

`Relationship between daily social media use and self-reported academic performance among Grade 12 students`

The second version is safer because it does not claim cause before data are collected.

#### 13.5 Ignoring Ethics

A topic may be researchable but still risky. Students must protect privacy, avoid harm, and collect only data that are needed. They should avoid asking for names unless absolutely required and approved. For most Grade 12 surveys, anonymous responses are safer.

### 14. What a Good Unit 1 Output Looks Like

By the end of Unit 1, a student or research group should have several possible topics. Each topic should show:

- area of interest
- specific issue
- possible variables
- possible respondents
- possible data source
- possible quantitative design
- feasibility notes

Example:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Area of interest | Learning continuity |
| Specific issue | Students may have different access to learning resources during class suspensions |
| Possible topic | Preferred learning resources during class suspensions among Grade 12 students |
| Possible variables | preferred resource, internet access, device access, perceived task difficulty |
| Possible respondents | Grade 12 students in one school |
| Possible data source | survey questionnaire |
| Possible design | descriptive survey |
| Feasibility note | Feasible if school allows anonymous survey and questions avoid sensitive personal data |

Another example:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Area of interest | Study habits |
| Specific issue | Students differ in how much time they spend reviewing lessons |
| Possible topic | Relationship between study time and quiz performance among Grade 12 students |
| Possible variables | study time, quiz performance |
| Possible respondents | Grade 12 students from selected sections |
| Possible data source | survey and quiz scores, if allowed |
| Possible design | correlational |
| Feasibility note | Needs teacher permission if actual quiz scores are used; self-reported quiz range may be safer |

These examples show that a research topic is not only a sentence. It is a set of aligned decisions.

### 15. Teacher Notes for Unit 1 Delivery

The most important teaching goal in Unit 1 is not memorization. It is helping students see the logic of quantitative research.

The core logic is:

`issue -> researchable problem -> variables -> respondents -> data source -> possible design`

If students can follow this chain, later units become easier.

The teacher should spend enough time on variables. Many later problems in PR2 come from weak variable understanding. If students cannot identify variables, they will struggle with titles, research questions, frameworks, hypotheses, instruments, and analysis.

The teacher should also normalize revision. Your first research topic is rarely the final one. Expect to narrow, revise, and improve your topic.

Good Unit 1 teaching should make research feel possible. Students do not need to master statistics yet. They need to understand that a broad concern can become a clear, measurable, ethical, and feasible study.
